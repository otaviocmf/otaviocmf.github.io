**By Henrique Zanoto**  
*October 9, 2026* | *China Agricultural University*

---

🐄 What does it take to produce quality beef where it is very hot?

During my internship, I spent weeks looking for the answer in breeds, numbers and the voices of people who live this every day in Brazil.

海南胜牛种业 (Hǎinán Shèng Niú Zhǒng Yè) works with beef cattle genetics in Hainan, a tropical region. As an intern, my job was to gather, organize and translate information about the Brazilian model of livestock production in hot climates, so the company could evaluate ways to produce premium beef under heat stress.

The result was a set of presentation-style materials in Portuguese, English and Chinese: a dossier on Brazilian heat-tolerant breeds, a full profile of the F1 Angus × Nelore, and two interviews with Brazilian industry professionals.

📊 Brazil in Numbers

Brazil has the second largest cattle herd in the world, behind only India: 238.2 million head in 2024, according to IBGE. That same year 39.3 million head were slaughtered, a record, and 2.55 million tonnes of fresh beef were exported.

What struck me most is where these cattle are. Mato Grosso (32.85 million head, 13.8% of the herd), Pará (25.6 million) and Goiás (23.2 million) are in the hot Center-West and North. Heat stress is one of the biggest constraints on productivity in the tropics, and about 80% of Brazil's cattle carry Zebu genetics. Nelore alone accounts for roughly 80% of beef cattle.

🧬 Breeds, Heterosis and Carcass Value

The most instructive part of the research was comparing how each breed copes with heat and what it delivers on the carcass.

I learned that the F1 does not come from a "mutation" but from combining two distant lineages, Bos taurus (Angus) and Bos indicus (Nelore). Each calf theoretically inherits 50% from each side, and the value lies in heterosis and complementarity. The commercial challenge is replicating the result reliably, since performance depends on parental genetics, nutrition, management, climate and health.

I also documented a price reference. A batch of male F1 calves, 8 to 10 months old and about 198 kg, sold for roughly R$ 2,835 per head (about R$ 14.32 per kg live weight). A regular Nelore male calf, 12 months and about 240 kg, was between R$ 3,350 and R$ 3,710 on August 7, 2026 in major producing regions.

🎙️ Learning from the People Who Live It

The most enriching stage was the interviews. Since the company's goal involved producing quality beef in hot climates, the questions ranged from how to choose an Angus sire to recommendations for a Chinese company that wants to raise Angus in the tropics without crossing with Zebu.

This first professional, from a feedlot-based operation, pointed to the newborn calf as the most critical phase: without the right care in the first hours, the impact lasts the animal's whole life. He added that a vaccination mistake or an outbreak of infectious keratoconjunctivitis can leave sequelae that destroy an animal's market value, and that because their animals are fully confined, they can breed heifers at ten months. In sire selection, he stressed calving ease and meat quality.

The second interviewee brought a management view. For the F1 he looks at calving ease, birth weight, weight gain, carcass quality, ribeye area, marbling, finishing, scrotal circumference, libido, feet and leg structure, and the stability of the breeder's herd. On supplementation he was direct: it does not automatically equal profit, and what matters is the chain intake → gain → cost → sale price → profit. He also noted that "uniformity is also a product."

💡 The Advice That Summarizes Everything

His main recommendation to the final question sums up the research well:

> "Don't try to turn Angus into Zebu; transform the system so Angus can express its potential in that environment."

He listed five fronts:

🔹 Adaptation-oriented genetic selection  
🔹 Pasture management  
🔹 Strategic supplementation  
🔹 Heat stress mitigation (shade, water, feed bunk design and handling times)  
🔹 Metrics-driven management, with the scale at the center of the operation, tracking daily gain, pregnancy rate, mortality, cost per arroba (@, 15 kg) and profit per hectare

🌱 From Breeds to Systems

I started the internship thinking it would be research about breeds. I left understanding it is research about systems: genetics, pasture, nutrition, health, people and economics working together.

I thank the team at 海南胜牛种业 for their trust, and the professionals who shared their time and knowledge in the interviews.
