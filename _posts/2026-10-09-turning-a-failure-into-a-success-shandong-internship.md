# Turning a Failure Into a Success

**By Filip Hamdan**  
*October 8, 2026* | *China Agricultural University*

This summer, I had the opportunity to spend several weeks working in China's agricultural and manufacturing sector as part of my MBA studies at China Agricultural University.

What began as an internship with Goodberry eventually took me through several different working environments in Shandong Province, from a modern agricultural park and a manufacturing factory to poultry farms and one of China's major international trade fairs.

The experience gave me something that a classroom cannot easily provide. It gave me a close look at how Chinese companies manufacture, sell and internationalise their products.

🌱 From Modern Agriculture to Manufacturing

My internship began with Goodberry, where I spent approximately two and a half weeks. I then visited Xinfa Modern Agricultural Industrial Park near Chiping.

Xinfa operates an interesting circular agricultural model. Its greenhouses produce strawberries and cherries, while largemouth bass are raised in indoor recirculating tanks. Waste heat and purified carbon dioxide from the group's industrial operations are used in the greenhouses, while waste from the fish operation can be reused as fertiliser.

The main lesson was surprisingly simple. What looks like waste in one operation can become a valuable input for another.


Shortly afterwards, my internship placement changed and I moved to Kaleter, a Chinese manufacturer of poultry farming equipment and environmental control systems.

That change turned out to be one of the most valuable parts of the experience.

🏭 Learning Manufacturing from the Factory Floor

At Kaleter, I was not limited to an office.

I spent time assembling equipment and components in the factory, learning about cages, feeding and drinking systems, environmental control equipment and manure management systems.

Working directly with the equipment changed the way I understood the relationship between manufacturing costs and international prices.

It is easy to discuss competitiveness in a business school classroom. It is different when you are standing on a production floor assembling the product yourself.

Kaleter competes internationally by offering a more cost competitive alternative to established European manufacturers. But the internship also showed me that price is only one part of international competitiveness.

A customer buying agricultural equipment is not simply buying a machine. They are also buying installation, technical support, spare parts, regulatory compliance and confidence that the equipment will continue working after the sale.

🌍 Seeing European Market Demands from China

One of my main assignments was researching the Serbian poultry market and the possibilities for Kaleter's expansion into Europe.

This involved looking at EU agricultural harmonisation, machine safety requirements, IPARD funding and the competitive landscape.

I also contacted a professor at the Faculty of Agriculture in Serbia to compare my findings with practical knowledge of the Serbian market.

One of my main conclusions was that entering a European market requires much more than offering a lower price.

For example, spare parts shipped from China can take weeks to arrive. Establishing a European spare parts stock could therefore become an important competitive advantage.

Similarly, farmers considering modern cage free systems have to look beyond the initial price of the equipment. The investment makes more economic sense when farmers can obtain a premium for cage free eggs or receive financial support for modernisation.

This made me realise that successful internationalisation is not simply about exporting a product. It is about adapting the entire business model to the market.

🏗️ From Construction Site to International Trade Fair

Another unusual part of the internship was working at an actual poultry house construction site.

I helped install feeder units, carry materials and assemble components for poultry houses destined for Europe.

This experience reinforced another important lesson. The product does not end when the machine leaves the factory.

For the customer, the real product is the completed and functioning poultry house.

Installation, technical support and after sales service therefore become part of the value proposition.


The final stage of my internship took me to Shanghai for VIV Select China, an international trade fair for the animal farming industry.

There, I helped prepare Kaleter's booth, install equipment, organise catalogues and certificates, demonstrate products and communicate with visitors from different countries.

I also spent time visiting other exhibitors and comparing their equipment, materials and presentation strategies.

Watching experienced sales staff interact with potential customers was particularly valuable. I saw how a conversation could develop from a simple question about a product into a discussion about technical requirements and eventually a potential quotation.

It demonstrated that a trade fair is not simply an exhibition.

It is one stage in a much longer sales and market entry process.


🇨🇳 What I Learned About Chinese Companies

The internship also changed my understanding of Chinese manufacturing.

Chinese companies are often discussed internationally in terms of their ability to compete on price. There is truth in that, but my experience showed me a more complicated picture.

Companies such as Kaleter are increasingly trying to combine competitive pricing with international certifications, engineering capabilities, customised production and overseas technical support.

At the same time, they operate in industries where regulation and customer expectations are becoming increasingly important.

For agricultural equipment, European welfare and safety standards are particularly significant. The transition from conventional cages toward cage free and aviary systems is creating both challenges and opportunities for manufacturers.

China is therefore not simply producing equipment for the domestic market. Chinese manufacturers are actively adapting their products and strategies to compete internationally.

🤝 The Personal Side of the Experience

Perhaps the most important lesson was adaptability.

During a few weeks, I moved between several different working environments, lived in a different part of China, worked with international colleagues and communicated in an environment where English was not always the main language.

I had to learn quickly, ask questions and become comfortable with not knowing everything immediately.

Working with colleagues from China, the Netherlands and other countries also gave me a practical lesson in international teamwork.

Different working styles do not necessarily have to be obstacles. When people share the same objective, those differences can become complementary.

The internship also gave me the opportunity to use my background in agriculture and my MBA studies in a practical environment.

Instead of studying international business only through cases and presentations, I could see how manufacturing, regulation, finance, logistics, sales and internationalisation interact in real business decisions.

🎯 From Observing to Contributing

Looking back, the most valuable part of the internship was the transition from simply observing a company to actually contributing to its work.

I assembled equipment in the factory, helped install systems on construction sites, researched potential export markets, supported the company at a job fair and represented the company at an international trade fair in Shanghai.

The experience showed me that international business is ultimately about connecting many different pieces.

A competitive product is important, but so are regulation, logistics, financing, after sales service, local market knowledge and relationships.

My internship in Shandong gave me a much clearer understanding of that reality.

It also strengthened my interest in international business and the role that Chinese companies can play in global agricultural markets.

For me, the journey from the factory floor in Shandong to an international exhibition hall in Shanghai was more than an internship.

It was an opportunity to see how ideas from an MBA classroom actually work in the real world.
